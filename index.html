How does a young bird know where to go?

During its first autumn migration, a young bird flies thousands of kilometres, often crossing seas and deserts, to a place it has never been to. The instructions to do so are in its DNA. We will move eggs between countries to find out what information the bird inherits from its parents.

See the two models

An ultimate test of the inheritance of migratory routes.

An instruction the bird is born with

Many songbirds migrate alone and at night, so nobody can show a young bird the way. The route must be partly inherited, but what exactly is passed on is still unresolved.

Is it a simple instruction, such as “fly in this direction for this long”? Or is it a destination, which would need an inherited map? Earlier experiments moved juvenile birds and gave ambiguous answers. Our project moves eggs instead, so the birds grow up far from where their parents bred.

Two models of the inherited program

Clock and compass

The genetic instructions set a direction and a flight time. A bird moved to a new place keeps its heading and ends up somewhere else.

Goal-area navigation

The genetic instructions point to a destination. A bird moved to a new place corrects its course and still reaches its population’s wintering area.

The birds

Meet the species

The great reed warbler (Acrocephalus arundinaceus) is a medium-sized (30 g) migratory songbird that breeds across Europe and Central Asia and winters throughout Sub-Saharan and Southern Africa. It depends strongly on reedbed habitats for successful reproduction.

Life in the reeds

Males are polygynous, meaning that a single male can mate with and hold a territory with several females. They feed on small invertebrates such as dragonflies, damselflies and spiders. A clutch holds 5 to 7 eggs, and both parents care for the young. Chicks leave the nest about 15 days after hatching and are fully independent around day 32.

Routes

Tracking with geolocators has shown that great reed warblers from different parts of the range take different routes. In a study of five western Palearctic populations (Spain, Sweden, the Czech Republic, Bulgaria and Turkey), autumn routes were similar in direction and largely parallel, and most Spanish, Swedish and Czech birds wintered along the Gulf of Guinea. Turkish birds instead wintered in East Africa, and together with Bulgarian birds they returned in spring over the Arabian Peninsula, making a counter-clockwise loop (Koleček et al. 2016).

Birds from Kazakhstan, at the eastern edge of the range, also winter in East Africa alongside the Turkish birds, even though genetic data suggest the species colonised Kazakhstan from the west. Their migration direction must therefore have changed substantially as the range expanded. In spring they leave Africa as early as February and spend up to two months at a staging site in southern Iraq before completing the journey, a two-step strategy not described before (Bensch et al. 2025).

The team

The people behind this project.

Staffan Bensch

Principal investigator, Professor

Staffan has studied great reed warblers since his PhD (Lund University, 1993), which drew on the long-term study at Lake Kvismaren that has been running since 1983. Today he leads an ERC-funded programme to uncover the genetic basis of songbird migration. Traits such as the urge to leave, the direction birds take and the length of their journeys are governed by unknown genetic mechanisms. To unravel them, he chose both Phylloscopus warblers and the great reed warbler as study species. In this project his role is coordination and fieldwork, especially at Kalimok station in Bulgaria.

Violeta Caballero-Lopez

Postdoctoral researcher

Violeta completed her PhD on the genetics of bird migration at Lund University in 2025. She focused on Phylloscopus warblers, combining genomic, molecular and phenotypic data to understand how small birds migrate. Before that, she spent several seasons on the great reed warbler project at Lake Kvismaren, where she came to know the species’ behaviour first-hand. In this project her role is to collect the eggs in Kazakhstan and to fit tracking devices on the birds in Bulgaria.
