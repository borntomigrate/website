How does a young bird know where to go?

Great reed warblers hatched from eggs we move between Kazakhstan, Spain and Bulgaria will be tracked by satellite on their very first migration. Their route will tell us what is written in their genes.

An instruction the bird is born with

Many young songbirds migrate alone, at night, to a wintering area they have never seen. Nobody shows them the way, so the directions must be inherited.

What exactly is inherited is still unresolved. Is it a simple instruction such as “fly this way, for this long”? Or is it a destination, which would require the bird to carry some sort of inherited map? Earlier translocation experiments have moved juvenile birds and gave ambiguous answers. This project does something that has not been done before: it moves the eggs.

Two ways to be born with a route

Clock and compass

The genes set a direction and a flight time. A bird moved to a new place keeps the same heading as its parents and ends up somewhere else.

Goal-area navigation

The genes point to a destination. A bird moved to a new place corrects its course and still reaches the family wintering area.

The answer matters for evolution too. If birds follow a compass heading, each breeding population needs its own direction genes. If they navigate to a goal, one set of genes can serve populations spread across a wide breeding range that share a wintering area.

The experiment

The first work package, WP1, is a translocation experiment. Populations that breed in different places but winter in the same part of Africa make the test possible.

  1. 2027

    Kazakhstan eggs. Eggs are collected from the Kazakh population and transported to the Kalimok field station in eastern Bulgaria.

  2. 2028

    Spanish eggs. The same procedure with eggs from Spain, a population with a simple southward route to western Africa.

  3. Hatching

    Hand-raising. Chicks hatch at the station and are raised alongside Bulgarian great reed warblers, using a protocol developed for aquatic warbler translocations in Lithuania.

  4. Aviaries

    Outdoor aviaries. Once they can fly, the young birds live in aviaries furnished to mimic their reedbed habitat.

  5. Release

    Satellite tags. A few days before the aviaries are opened, each bird gets a 2.0 g satellite transmitter. We then follow the first journey in real time.

Why the great reed warbler

The study needs a solitary long-distance migrant that is heavy enough to carry a tag and has populations with very different routes. This species fits.

Fieldwork so far

Kalimok field station in eastern Bulgaria is surrounded by a dense breeding population of great reed warblers. It is run in long-term collaboration with Dr. Pavel Zehtindjiev of the Bulgarian Academy of Sciences. In May and June 2024, a pilot study showed that chicks can be raised to independence, at about 32 days old.

An exploratory expedition to Kazakhstan has also been carried out to prepare the egg collection.
